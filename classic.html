Before the network: four little machines that still run the world

The training lab taught the gradient loop, and it is easy to leave it believing everything learns that way. Almost nothing does. Most of the decisions machines make about you today (the fraud hold on your card, the queue your support ticket lands in, the price band on your insurance) come from machines that are older, smaller and stranger: one that refuses to learn anything and just remembers, one that plays twenty questions, one that finds groups nobody labelled, and one straight line from 1958. This lab is those four, plus the trick for seeing high-dimensional data at all. Same world as the metrics lab: a reading screener’s learners, plotted by minutes read a day against comprehension.

Two neighbours. The training lab is the gradient story this lab deliberately is not. The metrics lab is how you would judge any of these machines once built. Either order works.

The machine behind this lab: a pegboard that asks its neighbours

In plain English: the simplest learning machine does not learn at all: it remembers every example and, for a new question, asks the nearest ones. Here the examples are dark and light pegs, the question is the lit peg, and an elastic band stretches round it until it holds k pegs; the colour with more pegs inside wins. With k = 1 one mislabelled neighbour decides everything. With k = 5 you get the local answer. With k = 15 you get the colour that is commonest on the whole board.
