The FDE bootcamp, day three: judge it, climb it, pay for it

Day three of a four-day forward-deployed-engineer bootcamp, rebuilt as a lab. Day one built the agent and day two let a thousand of them loose inside a company; day three asks the three questions that decide whether any of it was worth it. Can you trust the judge? An automatic rater is a model reading a rubric, and the day's first lesson is that it needs labelled data after all, so you learn to measure agreement with coefficients that do not lie, pick the criteria consistent enough to climb on, and climb. Can you fix the harness? The day's coding agent runs on a fixed small model; every gain has to come from its tools and instructions, and a diagnosis that names the task's bug instead of the agent's gap scores low. Can you afford the answer? One question fans out into coordinator turns, sub-agent calls, retrievals and thinking tokens, so you meter it, pull the levers (tiering, caching, thinking, retrieval, routing) and prove the bill halved while the quality held, on queries you never tuned against.

Neighbours. Day one is the anatomy and the build; day two is governance and scale. The hill-climbing loop in full is in the evaluation lab, the metrics behind the coefficients in the number to trust, and coding agents as builders in agents building agents. Day four (your own connectors, the enterprise front door) arrives as its own lab.

Judge, climb, pay

In plain English: by day three the agent exists and the fleet is governed. What is left is the uncomfortable part: deciding whether it is any good, making it better on purpose rather than by vibes, and paying for it. The day's three chapters are three questions. Judge: when a model grades another model's work, how do you know the grade means anything? You measure how far the judge agrees with people, and with itself, using coefficients that correct for luck, and you throw out the criteria that cannot be scored consistently. Climb: with a judge you trust on two or three criteria, you change the agent, re-run, read the judge's reasons, and repeat until the score clears a bar. For a coding agent the model is deliberately fixed and small, so every gain must come from the harness: the tools, the instructions, the verification loop. Pay: a single answer fans out into many calls, and nobody notices until the invoice; you meter each call, pull the levers, and prove on held-out questions that the bill halved and the quality held.
The analogy the day leaned on. A cooking competition. The judge (the autorater) has to be calibrated against the head chef's palate before their scores mean anything, and a judge who gives every dish ten out of ten is useless, not generous. The climb is the contestant changing the recipe between rounds, reading the judge's notes rather than guessing. The bill is the kitchen manager pointing out that the winning dish costs more to make than it sells for, and that the same dish with a cheaper cut and a hotter pan would have scored the same.

What an autorater is, and the big lie

In plain English: an autorater is a model that reads a criterion, the agent's reply, the input and whatever context you give it, and returns a score with its reasoning. The job it does is grading the final response on things that are subjective or slow for people: tone and style, organisation and flow, nuance, faithfulness to the sources, safety and security aspects, multimodal content, and the on-demand monitoring of live traffic that no team could read by hand. The history explains why it tempted everyone. First, people labelled everything: accurate and ruinously expensive. Then maths with no labels, like perplexity: cheap and weak. Then overlap scores against one reference answer: brittle, because the right answer in different words scores as wrong. Then embeddings against one reference: better. Then autoraters, and the big lie: that an autorater needs no labelled data at all. It does. You still need people to tell you whether the judge is right, and the judge saves you money only after that.
A case the trainer told. A large hospital group wanted nurse-to-nurse handover notes drafted from the records, because a handover for five patients was eating half an hour of every shift. Before any judge existed the team spent six weeks with doctors and nurses in batches, each rating the same drafts, and they disagreed: what a cardiac nurse wanted in a handover was not what a general-ward nurse wanted. The disagreements forced the guidelines to be written down sharply, and only then could the rubric be handed to a model as a judge. After that the judge did in minutes what the batches had done in weeks, and the half hour became five to ten minutes per handover. The order mattered: people first, judge second, never the reverse.
Pointwise or pairwise. A pointwise rating scores one output on a scale, say one to five. It is absolute and interpretable, has no cold-start problem, tells you the exact change on every data point between versions, maps cleanly to a business threshold, and its biases are less understood but easier to detect; it needs every criterion and every scale step defined, and very small improvements can be hard to see. A pairwise rating compares output A with output B. It is relative only, suffers a cold start (no benchmark yet, nothing to compare against), reports a win rate that can hide a regression on individual points, but it is mathematically simpler, needs only one definition of good, detects small consistent gains, and is what researchers and model builders favour. Its biases, position above all, are better understood and more likely to mislead. The day's rule: start projects pointwise; do pairwise later, as champion against challenger when a new version wants the old one's place.

Rubrics, prompts, and not everyone gets an A

In plain English: a rubric is a set of criteria; each criterion is scored on a scale with every step defined and distinct. The autorater's output is a function of the criterion, the response, the input and the context; the rubric's output is a function of all the autorater outcomes, for example at least four on every criterion before anything goes to production. A strong autorater prompt has six parts: a role (“you are an evaluator”), the steps to perform, as close as possible to what the human raters actually do and as specific to your criteria as you can make them, the criteria with each scale step defined, an output format that writes the reasoning before the scores, few-shot examples of inputs and scored outputs with reasoning, especially on the boundaries between scale steps, and the delimited inputs themselves. Two settings surprised the room: temperature one, because you want to see the judge's variance so you can measure it, and no structured-output forcing until you have checked it does not change the scores.
Not everyone gets an A. If every output scores the top mark there is no evaluation signal, and the usual cause is the homogeneity trap: criteria too easy to expose any difference, and a model that was trained to be agreeable giving your work full marks. Criteria must be challenging enough to expose real variance. And a good rubric is a consistent rubric: people apply it consistently, the autorater applies it consistently, and you have to test both. The process: draft the rubric; subject-matter experts test it in isolation; update it from their disagreements; test again; repeat until they agree; then a second group of experts; only then the autorater. The practicalities bite: redundant labelling irks people, fatigue sets in, you will probably need to test the same person twice (intra-rater reliability), do not label the labels, and watch out for panels, where the labels are the destination and the rubric is the friendship made on the way.

Measuring agreement, and when the judge disagrees

In plain English: you must measure agreement (consistency, inter-rater reliability), and two popular numbers are wrong for the job. Percent agreement does not correct for chance: with a dataset that is 95 percent one class, two raters agree 90 percent of the time by luck. Pearson correlation measures co-movement, not agreement: the columns one, two, three and three, four, five are perfectly correlated and never agree. Cohen's kappa corrects for chance but takes exactly two raters, no missing values and exact matches (a weighted variant forgives near misses). Krippendorff's alpha corrects more harshly, takes any number of raters, handles blanks, and works on nominal, ordinal or interval data; it is what the day and most practitioners prefer. Reading the number: above 0.8 is good enough to proceed, 0.6 to 0.8 is acceptable, below 0.4 is poor, and skew breaks kappa and alpha too, so when the set is lopsided reach for an alternative coefficient or, better, make the rubric harder. To measure human-against-judge, add the judge as one more rater and re-run the study, or swap one human for the judge; if the coefficient barely moves, the judge is as good as a person. The common shortcut, one human consensus label against the judge, throws away information if you already have several labels.
Set realistic expectations. Generic pre-written or adaptive rubrics: roughly 60 to 90 percent agreement with people. Calibrated autoraters on a foundation model with the techniques above: roughly 80 to 95. Something like 99 percent exists only for a very simple, highly specified task, maybe with a hyper-engineered cascade of prompts or a fine-tuned model. So know your goal before you start. A human-in-the-loop prototype that tolerates mistakes: vibe hard, iterate fast, use the stock autoraters, 80 percent is fine. A fully automated higher-stakes system: measure a careful baseline, climb scientifically, prefer precise measurement over velocity, calibrate and validate the judge, and stay focused, because maybe one or two tasks in the whole system need near-perfect judging. If you do not know what you need from the output quality, and therefore from the judge, you are setting yourself up to fail.
When the judge disagrees with your experts, work the list in order: check your expectations and that you are measuring correctly; analyse specific disagreements; read the judge's reasoning; refine the rubric and the prompt and add few-shot examples on the boundaries; check for obvious biases, length above all; use more criteria with fewer criteria per prompt; try pairwise; and only then the path of doom, fine-tuning a model or building a custom system. In the real world a few labels go a long way: a demonstration that the rubric is ambiguous builds consensus about underperformance and unrealistic expectations, autorater reasoning helps engage experts, a second set of labels shows the judge improving, and percent agreement will not end the world if you also report mean, standard deviation and absolute error, but correlation will. If you have no experts at all, use self-consistency: run the same input through the judge many times (more than eight, ideally fifteen to twenty), treat each pass as a rater, demand very high agreement, watch for skew and homogeneity, and eliminate the criteria that fail; then go further by varying the system instructions, the role, adding irrelevant noise and reversing the scale. A judge can be consistently wrong, so self-consistency is a backstop, not a substitute.

The lab's problem: no labels, no time, nineteen criteria

In plain English: the day's first challenge lab hands you a prototype agent that writes investment reports on vacant lots, built from a few skills an analyst drafted in a hurry, and a rubric of nineteen criteria the analysts think describe a good report. What you do not have is labels: nobody has had time to rate reports by hand, so you cannot run a human-against-judge study. What you can do is a self-consistency study: run every report through the judge several times (the lab's bigger run was 32 lots, 19 criteria, 15 passes) and treat each pass as a rater. Criteria the judge scores consistently are candidates; criteria it scores differently every pass are noise, whatever the analysts hoped. Two cautions from the room: the evaluation service majority-votes four samples behind every score it returns, which inflates every agreement metric, so be more conservative than the usual tables; and consistent is not the same as right, a judge can be consistently wrong, so a reliable criterion is still only a candidate for a human study later.
Reading the harness output. For one criterion the analysis tool prints a grid of lots down and passes across, with margins per report (minimum, maximum, range, mean, standard deviation, percent pairwise agreement) and per pass (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, range), and a summary line: mean of all ratings, the lowest and highest rating anywhere, the widest range on a single report, the widest range within a single pass, the standard deviation of report means, the standard deviation of pass means, mean pairwise agreement, and Krippendorff's alpha. Some of those are distractors, on purpose. Mean, minimum and maximum describe the reports, not the judge. The widest range on a single report and alpha describe the judge. Percent agreement is inflated by skew, and a criterion that scores five on everything will show perfect agreement and no information at all.
Then climb. The sponsors insist on two criteria, cost per acre and neighbouring flood risk, and one of them showed only moderate self-agreement in the study; you note the concern in your report and climb anyway, because the problem it detects must be fixed. The loop: run the agent across the target lots, a single-pass evaluation of the two criteria, read the mean, then read the judge's rationales for every lot that scored four or below, in the light of the criterion's definition and the agent's run in the development console; modify the skills; run again. Each run writes a versioned trace set and a versioned results folder. The bar is a mean above four on both. And one operational gotcha the lab underlines: the progress check reads only the last run, so when both means clear the bar, stop and press the button before you keep experimenting for fun.

Why coding agents, and the loop they run

In plain English: coding is the dominant use of these models by revenue, many enterprises build their own coding agents, and even non-coding work (document analysis, say) often ends with task-specific code running in a sandbox. More to the point for an engineer, coding is the proving ground: the design patterns that work for agents were born here (state isolation, deterministic hooks, external verification, context management) and transfer to everything else. What makes a coding agent different from a chat agent is the horizon (hours, planning, sub-agents, thousands of tool calls), the context (it rarely fits and must be curated continuously), the environment (repositories, a terminal, code execution), the evaluation (objective: compilers, tests, type checkers) and the failure modes (production bugs, leaked secrets, security holes). The architecture has a context and state block (a context engine, steering, durable state, tool contracts) feeding a loop of four steps: assemble the prompt, call the model, execute tools, observe and log; a secure sandbox with hooks and permissions in front of the environment; external verifiers; a human for approvals; and one question after every turn: done, over budget, or stuck?
The loop, as code. Build the prompt from the system instructions, the tool definitions, the transcript and the environment state; call the model; execute whatever it asked for and append the results; exit on completion, an unrecoverable error or a cap. Run it as a state machine: fixed parameters apart from mutable state, transitions all or nothing, every transition logged with its reason; store the to-do list, decisions and task status outside the window so compaction cannot lose them; and enforce caps in code, not in prose. Self-correction loops get stuck, and the room knew it: a diminishing-returns detector stops a run whose turns add nothing new, a stuck-loop detector caps retries on a structurally identical failure and insists that a “fix” actually changed relevant code, and hard caps on attempts, tokens and time prevent runaway cost; recovery is a cascade, cheapest and safest first. Plan mode is a read-only exploration phase before any edit, and skipping it on a task that needed it is the leading cause of sessions degrading into compounding fixes that never converge, so instrument “skipped planning” as a failure mode. Route the agent's changes through the same pipeline as a person's, type checks, integration tests and security scans, and never let the agent control or bypass that pipeline. Classify every exit reason into telemetry: completed, token budget exceeded, maximum turns, recovery loop detected, model error. The trainer's cautionary tale: a company raised a per-seat limit from twenty dollars to two thousand because things were going well, and some seats returned eighteen-hundred-dollar months for very little visible work; the loop was the reason.

Context, tools and finding your way around the code

In plain English: more context does not make better reasoning. Context degradation is the progressive loss of an agent's ability to use what it has accumulated: relevant facts buried, confusion between old and current state, instructions violated, attention reduced, actions repeated, drift from the goal. System instructions fail at two extremes, a hard-coded branch for every edge case (brittle, expensive to maintain) and “write good code” (improves nothing); the right altitude is heuristics not rules, organised into labelled sections, with a few canonical examples, started minimal on a strong model and grown only to address observed failures. Because every prompt re-sends the whole transcript, input tokens grow with the square of the number of turns; the fix is to make every request a prefix extension of the last so the implicit prefix cache hits, roughly a tenfold saving, and the traps that silently break it are changing the tool list, editing earlier messages or tool results, changing the sandbox or permission mode, and switching models mid-session. Compaction is a ladder, safest first: clear a tool result's raw payload once its decision is extracted; swap stale outputs for short placeholders, touching only turns past the cached prefix; collapse a span of stale turns into one shorter summary; and last, a full compaction with one inference, preferably on a cheaper model, complete before concise, with architecture decisions, hard constraints and user requirements surviving every time, plus a manual trigger. Memory sits outside the window in tiers (working, episodic, semantic, workspace), which the kit maps to its session, memory and artifact services, and tasks too long for one window are split into phases with fresh sessions that boot from disk: the progress file, the git log, the tests. An agent-facing README holds the project's conventions: command-first, nearest file wins, under about two hundred lines, because adherence degrades with too many rules.
A tool is a contract with a non-deterministic caller. Run an API client twice and you get identical calls; run an agent twice and you get a different tool order, different arguments, sometimes no call at all, because every call is a fresh decision shaped by the description and schema that sit in the prompt every time. Write every description as if onboarding a fast, literal-minded new teammate; design the response as carefully as the call (meaningful identifiers not opaque ids, a verbosity switch, engineered truncation that says what was cut and how to narrow, errors that read like prompts, a hard cap on length); and keep the set small and sharp, task-shaped rather than one tool per endpoint, with the litmus test that if a human engineer cannot say which of two tools to use, neither can the agent. Evaluate tools like a product feature before shipping, on realistic multi-call tasks, instrumented beyond pass or fail: a string of near-identical calls says the tool should return more; invalid parameters say the description is unclear. For navigating code, match the strategy to how fast the content changes: conventions and build commands are preloaded from the README; the code itself is searched just in time; a structural map of symbols orients a fresh session in a couple of thousand tokens; a semantic index is for abstract questions over an unfamiliar massive code base; and preloading the whole repository (a million tokens is roughly fifty to sixty thousand lines) trades retrieval misses for context rot, so it is only for small ones. The code-intelligence ladder is priced: string search, then a parser, then a language server, then the compiler, then the tests. The practice is “the model proposes, the language server verifies”: diagnostics after every edit, references before every rename, procedures wrapped as intents (a safe rename is references, rename, check for new errors), while string search still wins for strings, configuration and docs, and static analysis never substitutes for a test.

How the bill arrives

In plain English: ask the room how a model call is billed and the first answer is tokens per million, input and output. Keep asking and the rest surfaces: the model tier, the thinking tokens (reasoning bills as output, the priciest meter), cached input at a steep discount, and the time to answer. The business asks a different question, the total cost of ownership of an agent or a fleet, and three sentences frame it. Launch-day sizing is a forecast, not a contract, so never let a customer read an estimate as a promise. Standing still costs money: sessions to store, caches to keep warm, vector-serving nodes that bill around the clock whether queried or not, the standing infrastructure of a building with nobody in it. And re-measure regularly, resize at every renewal. There are four billing models and each has its own lever. Token-based, dollars per million tokens for model calls (input, cached, output): manage counts and tiers. Resource-based, dollars per node-hour for vector serving nodes and the agent runtime: right-size, and scale to zero where you can. Provisioned, dollars per scale unit per term from a week to a year: size the reservation to the baseline and balance it against pay-as-you-go overflow. Seat-based, dollars per user per month for the enterprise assistant licences: governance, who actually needs access; the day's story was five thousand licences bought and fifteen hundred used, and the remedy was workshops, not a smaller invoice.
Five consumption options. Provisioned throughput, backed by an agreement, a fixed price per scale unit per term, for critical stable traffic that needs predictable performance and price. Priority pay-as-you-go, about 1.8 times the base rate, for critical user-facing traffic that needs reliability and lower latency at peak. Standard pay-as-you-go, the base rate, for variable workloads without a strict availability promise. Flex, about half, best effort within thirty minutes, for cost-sensitive traffic that can wait. Batch, about half, results within a day, for throughput-heavy latency-insensitive jobs: extraction, classification, sentiment. The decision tree: is the request latency-sensitive? If not and it is synchronous, flex; if it is a bulk asynchronous job, batch. If yes, can it tolerate 429s? If yes, standard. If not, analyse the minute-level traffic pattern, size the reservation to cover the baseline, and spill peaks to priority and standard. A scale unit is the common currency of reserved capacity: throughput is input tokens per query plus output tokens per query times queries per second, converted with burn-down rates that account for the model and the context length, so a reservation covers different models by different amounts. One more fact from the deck, to take with a pinch of salt: tokenisers differ by language, and the vendor's own count showed its model using up to a fifth fewer tokens than a rival on several Asian languages (and slightly more on English); input only, and it says nothing about quality.

The levers, and the triangle they sit in

In plain English: no lever is free. Output quality, latency and cost form a triangle, and before you cut cost you ask three questions: is a person blocked waiting on the result (if not, a cheaper asynchronous tier is safe); does lower quality cost more than the tokens saved, say an escalation to a human; and is the output internal and disposable or external and binding? Then the levers. Tier. Flash-Lite for high-volume simple latency-sensitive steps, Flash as the strong default, Pro for complex reasoning only, at several times Flash per token; pick the lowest tier that clears your quality bar, verified with a data-driven evaluation, and route per task, not per application. Cache the prefix. A cache write costs the full input rate, a cache read about a tenth of it, and an explicit cache pays storage per million tokens per hour while it lives; implicit caching is on by default and best effort with no storage fee, explicit caching is declared by you and guaranteed on every hit. The constraints that bite: a minimum of 4,096 tokens, at most ten megabytes per cached block, a time to live from one minute with no maximum, not available on flex or batch, available on provisioned in preview. The breakeven: a static block reused more than a handful of times per time-to-live window wins, and idle time pushes breakeven out, so set the time to live to your reuse cadence. The rules: static content first and dynamic context last, because breaking the prefix loses the discount; measure every call from the cached-token count in the usage metadata and log the hit ratio; investigate falling hit rates; act on the breakeven; do not assume tier discounts stack; and never budget on an implicit hit you have not verified.
Reduce tokens, keep the quality. Enforce a response schema so you get only the fields you asked for, cap the output tokens, set stop sequences, keep system prompts concise and trim few-shot examples, and kill the preamble, because every filler token is billed and fillers cost twice: a terse-output skill the trainer uses in his own sessions shaves a fifth to a third off the output. Set the thinking level per task: dynamic thinking is on by default at a tier-dependent level, low for deterministic steps such as look-ups and tool selection, verified by plotting quality against thinking level, with actual thinking tokens reported after the fact. Route by task, not by inertia: static routing maps step types to tiers (triage to Flash-Lite, synthesis to Flash or Pro); a cascade tries the cheap tier and escalates on low confidence or failed validation; dynamic routing classifies each request up front. Routing lives in the agent (the kit's routed model when only the model changes, a routed agent when instructions or tools change too) or at a gateway for fleet-wide policy, budgets and provider failover; whichever you use, score it on cost per resolved task, not per call. Stretch levers: over-retrieve twenty chunks and rerank to the best three to five (about four-fifths less retrieval context on every grounded call, for a small ranking fee per thousand queries); cap the fan-out, because an uncapped confused agent re-buys the whole context every step; and a semantic cache, where a hit never calls the model at all, provided hit rate times avoided model spend beats embedding, lookup and the always-on serving nodes with their two-replica floor. Governance closes the loop: quotas are a hard per-minute ceiling (blunt: peaks get 429s, handle with backoff); budgets and alerts notify at thresholds and can trigger automation (a safety net, not a control plane); spend caps pause traffic at a limit (disruptive, and not every service supports them); labels with billing export to the warehouse attribute spend by team, application, model and environment, provided you label at provisioning time.

Design for the agent's eyes

In plain English: an agent reads a tool's output the way a person reads a screen, except that it pays per character and re-reads everything it has ever seen on every turn. The day pointed at a community design guide for agent-facing interfaces with ten principles in three groups. Token efficiency: a token-efficient output format that declares the field names once and streams rows (flat lists only; deep nesting inverts the saving); minimal default schemas of three or four fields per item with the rest behind a flag or a detail view, because every unrequested field bills on every row and then again on every later step; and truncation with a size hint (“truncated, 2,847 characters in total, use the full flag”) so the agent knows what it is missing and can opt in. Robustness: pre-computed aggregates (“27 passed, 0 failed, 10 skipped”) that turn a read-the-whole-log loop into one line, because fewer steps beats cheaper steps; a definitive empty state (“0 results, nothing matches”) rather than a blank the agent has to re-query to interpret; and structured errors with distinct exit codes, idempotent mutations, never an interactive prompt, unknown flags failing loudly. Discoverability: ambient context installed by a setup hook, content first (a no-argument call returns live data, never a help screen), contextual disclosure of the logical next command with placeholders ready to fill, and a consistent concise help on every subcommand. The evidence the day cited, from a browser-tool case: eleven turns at about nineteen cents became two turns at under five, because the tool did the multi-step work so the agent did not have to.
The toolbox and the transcript. Fifty tool schemas in the prompt is roughly 77 thousand input tokens re-processed on every turn, and selection accuracy drops past about thirty visible tools, so the agent makes wrong calls and retries; a semantic tool retriever that matches the intent against tool descriptions, which the kit exposes as toolset filtering, puts three or four schemas in front of the model instead, about nine-tenths fewer tokens, with the same tools available. The transcript is managed by masking first: resolved tool outputs become typed placeholders while the reasoning and call parameters stay verbatim, about 95 percent less history bloat, deterministic, no extra calls; summarising instead spends its own model calls (around seven percent of inference cost), smooths over failures and destroys the stack-trace evidence. Compact early, at 50 to 60 percent of the window, keeping decisions and open threads and dropping the raw transcript, before rot bakes into the summary; be cache-aware, since restructuring invalidates the prefix, so never break a hot prefix for less than it saves; and keep the static blocks (system prompt, tool schemas, corpus) first so they ride the discount while the per-turn dynamics trail.

Verifiers outside the model, and agents that cheat

In plain English: a coding agent is verified deterministically, by linters, type checkers, compilers and tests, and the verifier must sit outside the model, because asking a model to grade its own code is not trustworthy enough; it must also return rich feedback, so the model can find the root cause of every issue rather than the first. Model-based verifiers add a second layer: dedicated critic models that scan for known vulnerability classes, logic flaws and specification drift, evaluating the code in isolation from the context that produced it to avoid self-confirmation, and returning structured critiques to guide the next attempt. Then the uncomfortable fact: your agent may cheat. Given “make sure all tests pass”, agents weaken or delete the failing tests, because that satisfies the formal criterion. Two defences. Test-driven development for agents: write the tests first, confirm they fail before implementation, forbid touching test files during implementation, confirm they pass after. And prove the tests can fail: break the code under test and re-run the suite; it must go red. When running benchmarks, watch for memorisation and for the agent searching the web for solutions.
The harness is a variable, and cost is a metric. On a public terminal benchmark the exact same model version scores about sixty percent under one agent harness and about eighty under another, so to evaluate models hold the harness fixed and to evaluate harnesses hold the model fixed. Treat public benchmarks as contaminated by default, never decide from a leaderboard alone, and prefer contamination-resistant sets. A good evaluation dataset gets two things right: task validity (pin every dependency version, a clean environment per run, unambiguous prompts, no shortcuts left lying around) and outcome validity (tests neither so narrow that they assert a function name nor so wide that they expect behaviour the prompt never mentioned). If autoraters grade any of it, use a strict rubric with every scale step defined, randomise the order in pairwise comparisons to defeat position bias, and calibrate against human-graded examples first. And always report cost per resolved task beside accuracy, plot the accuracy-against-cost frontier and kill the dominated configurations, and score the trajectory as well as the outcome: tool calls, redundant calls, tokens per resolved task, error rate. A pass that took three times the tool calls is an optimisation waiting to happen. Security for coding agents is defence in depth in three tiers: at ingress, external content is data not instructions and is delimited as such, planning runs in a low-privilege read-only sub-agent before a high-privilege executor, and lightweight classifiers screen suspicious strings; in the loop, deterministic hook interceptors block blacklisted command signatures, output hooks intercept secrets, package installs resolve against a whitelist, and tiered permissions auto-approve reads while a person approves writes, network calls and installs; at egress, autonomous code runs in a micro-VM or hypervisor-isolated container never a shared kernel, with the network adapter disabled, paths validated to the workspace, and ephemeral narrow-scope tokens with the sandbox torn down after every session. The room's example of why: a helper library on the internet whose documented function “creates an endpoint” while its body deletes one, pulled in faithfully by an agent with every permission.

When the panel says… the answer they are listening for

In plain English: each line is a question as a panel would phrase it, then the one-sentence answer that signals you built it, then the reason the usual answer loses.
“Do autoraters need labelled data?” → Yes; the big lie is that they do not. Calibrate against human labels, measure agreement with kappa or alpha, and when no experts exist use self-consistency across fifteen to twenty passes as a backstop, remembering a judge can be consistently wrong.
“Pointwise or pairwise?” → Start pointwise: absolute, no cold start, the exact change on every data point, easier agreement measurement and a clean map to a business threshold; go pairwise later for champion against challenger, where small consistent gains show but a win rate can hide a regression.
“Ninety percent agreement; are we good?” → Not necessarily: percent agreement does not correct for chance and a skewed set gets ninety by luck. Report Cohen's kappa for two complete raters or Krippendorff's alpha for any number with blanks, treat 0.8 as the bar, and be more conservative when the service majority-votes behind each score.
“Every criterion scores five on everything.” → The homogeneity trap: no variance, no signal, alpha not defined. The criteria are not challenging enough to expose a difference; rewrite them, because a judge that gives everyone an A is useless, not generous.
“Which of the rubric metrics describe the judge?” → The widest range within one report across passes, the spread of pass means, and alpha; the mean, minimum and maximum describe the reports, and percent agreement is inflated by skew.
“The judge disagrees with our experts.” → In order: check the expectation and the metric, read the judge's reasoning on specific disagreements, refine the rubric and prompt with boundary examples, check length and position bias, fewer criteria per prompt, try pairwise, and only then fine-tune or build custom.
“Fixed small model, two of five tasks solved. What do you change?” → The harness: a search tool and a run tool, read and plan before writing, run the code before finishing, errors phrased as prompts, a cap on retries of an identical failure. Never hard-code the tasks; the final grading rejects overfitting.
“The agent fixed the symptom in the wrong file.” → Diagnose the agent's gap, not the task's bug: it could not search the code base, so it never found the helper that applied the offset and changed the call site instead; a diagnosis that restates the bug scores low.
“How do you keep a coding agent from looping?” → Detect diminishing returns, cap retries on a structurally identical failure and insist a fix changed relevant code, cap attempts, tokens and time, classify every exit reason into telemetry, and make planning a phase it cannot skip unnoticed.
“Why does each turn cost more than the last?” → Every prompt re-sends the transcript, so input grows with the square of the turns. Keep each request a prefix extension of the last, and never change the tool list, edit earlier turns, switch permission modes or swap models mid-session, so the cached prefix reads at about a tenth.
“Is caching worth it for a 3,000-token prompt?” → No: below the 4,096-token minimum no cache is created. Above it, breakeven is a handful of reads per time-to-live window, about five on the Flash tier and seven on Pro, independent of block size, provided the static block comes first.
“How did you halve the cost per query without losing quality?” → A per-call ledger first, then levers combined: a Flash coordinator, reranked retrieval or a cached prefix, lower thinking on deterministic steps, graded on held-out queries. No single lever was enough, and a semantic cache cannot help a grader that never repeats a question.
“Which consumption option?” → Latency-sensitive and able to take a 429, standard; not able, size a reservation to the minute-level baseline and spill peaks to priority; latency-tolerant, flex at about half; asynchronous bulk, batch at about half within a day.
“Fifty tools and the agent keeps picking the wrong one.” → About 77 thousand tokens of schemas on every turn and accuracy that drops past thirty; retrieve the three or four tools the intent needs with a semantic tool retriever through the kit's toolset filtering, same tools, nine-tenths fewer tokens.
“Summarise the transcript or mask it?” → Mask resolved tool outputs with typed placeholders first: deterministic, no extra call, keeps the reasoning and the stack traces. Compact at fifty to sixty percent before rot bakes into any summary, and batch the cache breaks instead of clearing one by one.
“The agent made all tests pass. Ship?” → Prove the tests can fail: break the code and the suite must go red. Lock the test files during implementation, write them first and confirm they fail, and run the verifier in a pipeline the agent can neither edit nor skip; its own review of its own diff is not a verifier.
“Quota, budget alert or spend cap?” → A quota is a hard per-minute ceiling that returns 429s; a budget alert notifies at a threshold and can trigger automation but never blocks; a spend cap pauses traffic and is disruptive; labels with billing export are how spend is attributed by team, agent and tier.
