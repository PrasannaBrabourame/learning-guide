The FDE bootcamp, day four: federate it, fence it, plug it in

Day four of a four-day forward-deployed-engineer bootcamp, rebuilt as a lab. The first three days built an agent, governed a fleet of them and proved the fleet was worth its bill; day four is the recap-and-level-up day, where everything is remapped onto the company's front door for AI: the enterprise app that employees open, with its hundred and fifty connectors, its identity choices and its guard rails. Life of a query: search-first became agent-first, ingestion became federation, and a question now goes through classification, rewriting, fan-out, de-duplication, reranking and grounding before anyone sees an answer. Who gets in: the identity decision a customer has to live with for years, the sign-in flows behind it, and the places each one breaks. Screen and fence: Model Armor on prompts and replies, sensitive-data policies at the connector, keys you hold, a perimeter around the project, org policies even an admin cannot bypass, and the logs that prove it. Plug it in: the API behind the app, the authorisation flow a custom portal has to copy, and when to bring your own tool server instead of waiting for a connector.

Neighbours. Day one is the anatomy and the build, day two governance and scale, day three the judge and the bill. The rollout view of the same front door (licences, connectors, tenancy) is in rolling it out to a whole company, and the estate-wide controls in govern and secure an estate of agents.

Federate, fence, plug in

In plain English: the first three days were about one agent, then many agents, then whether they were any good. Day four asks where all of that lives. The answer the trainer kept returning to is the front door: one enterprise app that every employee opens. Behind it sit the agents you built, the data in a hundred and fifty other systems reached through connectors, and the controls a security team will ask about before a single user is licensed. Why one door rather than ten agents handed out individually? Because ten agents, each with its own login, its own data permissions and its own screening, is ten things to get wrong; the front door makes identity, screening, perimeter and logging a property of the building, not of each room. The day's four chapters follow a query inwards. How a question travels through the app (life of a query). How the person asking it was let in (who gets in). What happens to every word on the way in and out (screen every word). What stops data leaving the building at all (the perimeter). Then the day turns the door around: how a customer's own portal or own tool server plugs into it (plug it in), and what to do when it goes wrong.
The analogy the day leaned on. An office building. Reception checks who you are (identity: the company's own directory, or a pass issued by someone the company trusts). The badge decides which floors open for you (authorisation, decided live at each floor rather than copied onto the badge). Security screens what you carry in and what you carry out (Model Armor on prompts and replies, sensitive-data policies on documents). The walls, the one staffed entrance and the geo-fence decide where a visitor may even arrive from (the service perimeter). The safe holds what the company stores under its own key (customer-managed keys). The visitor log proves afterwards who came and went and stops nobody (logs). And the goods entrance round the back is the API, where the company's own portal and its own tool servers plug in, following the same rules as the front.

Search-first became agent-first

In plain English: the enterprise app's first architecture was a search engine with a model bolted on. The user interface called an assistant server, and the server ran retrieval: embeddings, permission checks, search. Everything it could retrieve had to have been ingested first. Ingestion jobs copied the data from the source system into the provider's indexes and document stores, and copied the source's access lists too. That is the architecture most of the room had built themselves as retrieval-augmented pipelines. It had a flaw the trainer called effort against return. Every source meant copying its data, reworking its schema, rebuilding indexes, keeping a stale copy fresh (hours at best, usually a day), and paying for all of it. The second architecture is agent-first. The assistant server hands the question to a root agent. Every connector is a dedicated agent beneath it: a search agent, a calendar agent, a ticket agent, many others. All are built on the agent development kit, and they talk to the source systems live, through a federated search service, instead of to a copy. Federation arrived with the tool protocol the room already knew, and it is what let the connector catalogue grow from a few dozen to over a hundred and fifty in a year: a connector became a thin federated adapter rather than an ingestion pipeline. Two ways an agent beneath the root reaches the conversation: as a tool, where the caller sees only the tool's output, and by context injection, where an agent is allowed to modify the shared context programmatically.
What is coming. Two architectures sit beside each other on the current slide. A planner-plus-skills arrangement flattens the tree: a skill-and-model selection step picks what the root agent should load for this question, then the root agent calls the search API, the workspace API, the connector platform and the federated search service directly. And long-running agent nodes, in private preview. A planner generates code for skills and tools. A sandboxed virtual machine holds a skill cache. The nodes can run a task for up to a day, reaching first-party APIs, third-party APIs and tool servers, a browser, and tool-protocol apps. Access is by request through your account team, and the best practices are still being written. For most use cases in the room it is not needed yet. But when a customer asks for a task that takes hours, this is the answer, rather than a chatbot kept awake.

Try it: the three clocks

Try this: one real-shaped request, thirty-three seconds end to end, laid out as lanes. The lanes are initialisation, a classifier, a skill selector, a web prefetch, and a fetch of the tool list from a tool server. Then the root agent's first turn, the tool call, and the root agent's second turn reading a 55-kilobyte tool response. Three clocks are read off it: time to first token (the first chunk of anything, thought or text), time to first answer token (the first chunk of the actual reply, chain-of-thought excluded) and time to last token. Pull the levers and watch which clock each one moves.
in real lifeA restaurant. Time to first token is when the waiter first speaks to you; time to first answer token is when the first plate lands; time to last token is when dessert is cleared. Streaming the kitchen's chatter to your table makes the first one earlier and changes nothing about the food. Stopping the chef from re-reading a fifty-page order makes the last one earlier.

Two kinds of people, two kinds of pass

In plain English: two personas touch the app and they sign in to different places. Admins sign in to the cloud console, the command line, the API or infrastructure-as-code to configure it. They need an identity that can log in to the cloud, and IAM roles for admins. An existing cloud customer adds them to the project through the directory it already has. A greenfield customer first sets up the foundations: an organisation node, a project, billing, a directory. End users sign in to the app's web page to search, build agents and run actions. They need three things: an identity that can log in to the app, the enterprise-user or restricted-user role, and a licence, bought on the billing account and assigned at a project and a location. For the end users there are exactly two kinds of pass. Cloud Identity is the provider's own directory. The customer syncs people and groups from whatever directory they run (on-premises, or a cloud identity provider) into accounts in that directory, through a directory-sync tool or a gallery app. Those accounts work for every product the provider makes. Workforce Identity Federation is bring-your-own: the customer federates from a third-party identity provider into a workforce pool, nothing is synced or created, and the pass works only for specific cloud products. Both support single sign-on through the customer's identity provider; the difference is whether an account exists on the provider's side.
How each sign-in works, and who decides what. With Cloud Identity, a pre-step configures a single-sign-on profile with the third-party provider and the directory sync. Then the user opens the page. The login service asks the organisation's identity provider to authenticate them and receives the assertion. It checks the account is valid and matches the login profile, activates a session and issues a token. The user is in. With federation, a pre-step configures the identity providers against a pool. Then the user opens the page and requests a token from their provider, which returns it with their attributes. The token is posted to the security token service, which evaluates it against the pool and hands back a cloud token. The user is in. Note the extra hop. The division of labour is the same in both. Authentication (who you are) belongs to the identity provider. Authorisation (what you may do) belongs to IAM, which reads attributes and group membership either from the synced directory objects or from the token the security token service minted. The licence decides whether the app will serve you at all. And under federation the source system decides, live, which documents you may read. A 403 from the source is passed straight back, and the app never maintains an access list.
How the recommendation flipped. The product launched at the end of 2024 with ingested connectors only. Within months, federation became the recommended way to use any Microsoft connector. Ingesting from that suite had a limitation with Cloud Identity: the access lists carried group identifiers that only the customer's own directory could resolve. So the pool had to federate with that directory. By the autumn of 2025 federated connectors were the recommended approach generally, and that reversed the identity advice. With a federated connector the user signs in again at the source. No access-list mapping or syncing is needed, and permissions are checked in real time. So the directory you pick only has to protect the app itself, and the provider's own directory does that with the least friction. Ten or more new federated connectors followed, then advanced sharing and per-app access controls, and by spring 2026 Cloud Identity is the recommended default, with federation the exception.

Identity made easy, except when

In plain English: the day's headline was use the provider's directory for everything, almost. It supports federated connectors (no access lists to worry about: a federated connector authorises the end user at the source, so the app's identity provider only has to protect the app). It supports the mobile app, which today accepts only the provider's directory or federation with the Microsoft directory. It supports sharing notebooks and agents with type-ahead, because the directory knows everyone's name; a federated pool would need a provisioning sync to do the same. And it supports immutability. People change their email addresses. That is benign for a directory account and catastrophic for a federated pool whose subject is the email: the person becomes a new principal, and their licence, chat history, notebooks and shares stay with the old one. The real reasons to still federate are three. Ingestion connectors from the Microsoft suite: diminishing, but they still use access lists, so the pool needs that directory for the group identifiers. A customer who requires a session timeout: the free tier of the directory fixes it at sixty minutes. Configuring it sits behind a premium tier at about six dollars per user per month, which on ten thousand users is a real line in a budget. And because the customer said so, who is mostly right. The former fourth reason, custom applications calling the API with their own tokens, is now fully supported by the directory and was struck through on the slide.
The configuration, in one breath each. Cloud Identity: start from a tenant with a verified primary domain. Create the single-sign-on profile with SAML, and copy its entity id and assertion-consumer URL into a new application at the identity provider. Note the name-id mapping, the login and logout URLs and the entity identifier; download the provider's certificate and finish the profile. Set up one-way inbound user and group provisioning by creating a robot account with a custom admin role (create, read, update, delete on users and groups, read on organisational units). Align the user's primary email with the attribute used as the name id, and derive the group email from the group's display name and your primary domain. Assign the users and groups, switch provisioning on, and assign the profile to the synced groups. Then, in the cloud console, configure the app to use the directory at the chosen location and grant the enterprise-user role. Federation: create the pool and provider at the organisation level, and register an application at the identity provider, noting the issuer URL and client id. Create the client secret and add the groups claim to the id token. Map the subject (the product's preference is the email), the groups and the display name. Create a provisioning tenant with a claim mapping aligned to the subject; save its base URI, tenant id and security token. Give those to a provisioning application at the identity provider, whose tenant URL is the base URI with the provider's required suffix. Check that the external id of users and groups is sourced from the object id. Assign users and groups, start provisioning (the first sync can take hours on a large organisation), and switch the provider to use the provisioned data. You do not memorise this; you keep the documentation open, because every identity provider spells it differently.
Watchpoints for the federated path. Map every user identifier an ingestion connector will need (the principal name the user logs in to the third-party application with, as an extra attribute), or access lists will not match. Migrating a SAML provider to OIDC is a new provider under the same pool and an update to the application configuration. The web login looks the same. Licences follow the subject, with no duplication if both are configured correctly. Connectors are unaffected. IAM, the perimeter and sharing all use principal sets keyed on the pool, not the provider. OIDC is required for the mobile app with bring-your-own identity. And two protocol limits bite without provisioning. When a user belongs to more than a hundred and fifty groups, their groups are redacted from the token entirely. And a pool is stateless, so it cannot offer auto-complete for names when sharing. A provisioning sync of users and groups into the pool fixes both.

The query's path through the controls

In plain English: a query enters through authentication (the directory or the federated provider), then authorisation (IAM), then the licence check, and only then does it become a query. Before it reaches the app's engine it passes Model Armor, which can flag it, redact it, block it or let it through. The app sends it through the Discovery Engine API, the backbone of the whole product. (The name is a fossil from the search-first era; the app it fronts is now described as search and assistant.) From there it goes into the provider's internal orchestration. That reaches the connectors, each running in a tenant project of its own, and out to the external systems. Under all of it sit the storage controls: the indexed content and the service storage under customer-managed keys where you have asked for them, and the conversation history, which keeps prompts and responses for sixty days. The point of drawing it is to see that there are two places to put a sensitive-data check, on the query and reply at the app, and on the documents at the connector, and they catch different things.
Two prompt-injection scenarios set the tone. A user tells the assistant it is their research assistant, that they want to help people quit a drug, and that it must therefore ignore its safety filters and explain the recipe from a television series. The model's built-in safety declines, and offers the science of addiction and treatment programmes instead. That is good. But a customer who wants to see that someone tried, or to show nothing at all, needs Model Armor in front of the model, with logging on. And a user asks what the secrets in their cloud drive are, and gets two code words from two documents, correctly, because the source's access lists were messy and the documents were shared too widely: the model did nothing wrong, the data classification did. The second scenario is why document labels matter: the drive and the Microsoft suite both let an organisation label a file confidential, and a content policy can block on the label itself.
Model Armor is four detectors behind one allow-or-deny policy. A content-safety model: harassment, dangerous content and the other categories. The sensitive-data-protection service: personal data by template, a hundred and fifty built-in information types plus custom ones. A prompt-safety model: prompt injection and jailbreak attempts. And the provider's anti-virus and safe-browsing checks: malicious files, unsafe links. It is model-agnostic and sold on its own for other applications, but inside the enterprise app it is included in every edition at no extra charge, switched on from the app's security settings, and available today for apps in the US and EU locations. The best practices the day listed. Use the advanced sensitive-data setting with a custom inspection template, because the basic one is for demos. Enable Model Armor's own logging, because the app's log records the verdict but not the severity, nor whether it was the prompt or the response. Use separate templates for prompt and response. Enable multi-language support, or a jailbreak in another language walks through. Keep inspect-only for a dry run; it is set through the API. Set the detectors' confidence to high, then audit the request-and-response logs regularly. Part two. Disable prompt-injection detection on the response template: a reply that quotes an email saying “ignore my previous message” is not an attack. Try the latest rather than the stable detector models. Rely on the sensitive-data service for any custom allow or deny list. Use inspect-only as a last resort, for a customer who cannot tolerate blocking.

Content policies: the scan at the connector

In plain English: the sensitive-data-protection service on its own discovers, classifies and de-identifies data anywhere: a hundred and fifty built-in information types, custom types, machine-learned document classification, and de-identification by tokenising, format-preserving encryption, masking or bucketing, feeding the security command centre and the data catalogue. Inside the enterprise app it appears twice. Inside a Model Armor template, as the inspection template that screens prompts and replies. And, newer and the subject of the day's lab, as a content policy attached to a connector, which scans the documents a federated connector fetches, before the model sees them. A content policy handles larger files, document labels from the provider's suite and sensitivity labels from the Microsoft suite, allow and block rules, over two hundred classifiers including image safety, and optical character recognition and object detection on images. It is configured per connector (shared across several or dedicated to one, for example different rules for the document library and the mailbox), per app for files uploaded to the assistant, and per project for the notebook, because notebook uploads cannot be configured per app. It does not yet apply to ingested connectors, it does not apply to file uploads (Model Armor and the per-app policy cover those), and output posted through actions is not covered by anything. It was in preview in the summer, with general availability to follow. Best practices: test text examples in the in-line tester during creation, and enable the enhanced logging to the data warehouse so you can report how often content matches the policy.
Why the connector, not just the app. The trainer put a question to the room: the human-resources team has a spreadsheet of employees in the drive and wants to use it through the app; the app has a Model Armor template with the sensitive-data inspection configured; is that enough? The room said yes, if the information types are configured correctly. The trainer's answer was a different question: where does the scan happen? The prompt is checked at step one. The query is rewritten and the federated connector returns the spreadsheet at step two. If the only check is the response template at step five, the spreadsheet has already passed through the internal engine, into the model's context, and possibly into the sixty-day conversation history, before the response check sees the reply. A content policy at the connector is step three: it blocks or redacts the document before the model reads it. The same logic covers the other gap in the slide's small print. Data arriving through connectors is not scanned by Model Armor at all, and indirect prompt injection hidden in a retrieved document is not detected by it. What sits at the connector is the content policy.
Limits worth memorising. Model Armor's token limits per filter: about ten thousand tokens for prompt injection, content safety and the child-safety filter, about a hundred and thirty thousand for sensitive data. Blocked queries do not count against the app's service-level agreement whether the template fails open or closed. The compliance certifications of the two products together are the intersection, and Model Armor carries the healthcare one today. Document screening on upload reads the text of PDF, CSV, plain text, and the three office formats. Metadata is scanned; images embedded in a PDF are not scanned by the injection and safety modules. Files over four megabytes are skipped and fail open. Rich-text files under fifty bytes are rejected. For uploads overall. A content policy takes plain text to ten megabytes and rich documents to thirty, configurable to fail open or closed above that. Model Armor gives partial cover up to four megabytes and about five hundred kilobytes of text. Images get safety, text recognition and objects. Video and audio are on the roadmap. The practical rule the trainer gave from scars: shard large files before you do anything with them.

Keys, the fence, the rules from head office, and the logbook

In plain English: four controls sit outside the model entirely. Customer-managed encryption keys: everything the provider stores is already encrypted with its own keys, and data in transit is encrypted and re-keyed by the provider on every hop, so this is about customers who want to hold the key for what is stored at rest. Three ways: a key the customer manages in the cloud key service, a key generated and held in a cloud hardware security module, or an external key manager where the key never lives in the cloud. The data the app stores under it: access tokens, indexed content, the authorisation data for actions, and the user session data (prompts and responses, kept sixty days). The order of operations matters and cannot be undone. Register the keys in the project's settings first. Select the key when the data store is created. Assign that store to the app. Make sure the engine's service agent can encrypt and decrypt with the key. The limitations. Non-global regions only: a US or EU location, not global. The key on a data store cannot be changed or deregistered. Rotating the key version breaks a third-party connector on a single-region key, until support lands. More than one key per project needs a quota increase. A data store created before the key was registered can never be protected by it. And analytics is not compatible with rotation. The trainer's framing: the app itself stores little; under federation the data stays at the source and the transit encryption is the provider's job; customer keys matter when you ingest, because then you hold a copy.
The service perimeter. VPC Service Controls draws a boundary around the projects holding the data and controls how data moves across it; its one goal is to prevent exfiltration that nothing else stopped (IAM, network controls and org policies all passed, and the data still should not leave). Ingress and egress rules say which identities, from which sources, may perform which operations on which services inside, and in which direction. The scenario the day drew: the app's web endpoint is public and reachable from the internet, and the customer wants it reachable only from the corporate network. A perimeter with an ingress rule allowing only traffic from the VPN range does that, at no extra charge. One warning: a top-level access level on the perimeter is not recommended, because it takes precedence over the ingress rules. The example everyone remembers is the geo-fence: a fully authorised employee on a managed laptop opens the app from a hotel abroad and gets a generic something-went-wrong, and only the logs say blocked by the perimeter. Two callouts: grounding on public web search inside the app cannot be blocked by a perimeter, and the API's service name covers both the app and the notebooks. And the perimeter blocks actions and data stores that leave the project, sending a message or creating a ticket, until the specific action is allow-listed through the egress policy. You can add the perimeter after the app exists; the trainer's line was build the house first, put up the fence later.
Org policies for connectors. Three constraints were designed for one risk: a malicious or careless app admin exfiltrating data by creating a connector nobody approved. They give the security team the ability to restrict connector creation from above, inherited down the resource hierarchy (organisation, folder, project, resource), so that even an admin with full project rights is refused. Allowed data sources restricts which connector types may be deployed; enabled by default for projects inside a perimeter; recommended on; it applies at creation and is not retroactive; it covers well-known domains. Disable custom tool-server connectors prevents data leaving to a bring-your-own tool server; enforced on every project by default regardless of the perimeter; applies at connection creation; switch it off only when you use such servers. Allowed egress domains restricts the fully-qualified domain names tool servers and connectors may reach; the admin can only connect to the company's own ticket domain. It is enabled by default for perimeter projects. It is needed when a source lives on a custom domain, or you want to pin the tool-server URLs. In a perimeter project, allowing a bring-your-own tool server means switching off the second constraint and listing the server in both of the others. The same mechanism is what blocks a training project from reaching anything outside the day's lab: the policy is set on a parent project, and every child inherits it.
The logbook. Four kinds of log, and only one is on by default. Admin activity audit logs (who configured what) are always on and cannot be switched off. Data access audit logs (who read what) are off; enable them. User prompt logs, the app's own log of what users asked and what agents did, are off; enable them, choose the retention (thirty, sixty or ninety days, priced accordingly) and sink them somewhere cheaper for anything longer. Model Armor verdict logs are off; enable them, because the app's own log lacks the severity and whether it was the prompt or the response. The warning on the slide: sensitive data is not filtered out of the usage logs, so if the logs flow into a central data lake, an extraction job must mask them first. The agent log answers a question security teams ask: what actions are my users taking? The entry for an agent action records the agent's name, the action and its arguments (send a message, with the subject, the body and the recipients). And a low-code agent respects the admin's configuration: users can only enable the connectors and actions the admin allow-listed.

The goods entrance: the API behind the app

In plain English: the web app and the mobile app are good, and customers do not stop there. They want the assistant inside the portal their staff already live in. They want to reuse the ready-made connectors (the document library, the tracker, the service desk) from their own code. And they want to call it programmatically from agentic workflows. All of it goes through the Discovery Engine API, with client libraries and REST, and a licence per user who calls it. Two methods matter. The streaming assist method on the engine's assistant resource is the recommended one: it streams a conversational, multi-turn exchange in real time, exactly what the app's own front end uses. The search method on a serving configuration returns results directly from indexed content, for the narrower cases where you want a results list rather than a conversation; its older streaming-summary sibling is deprecated for enterprise apps.
The limitation everybody hits. A federated connector only works for a user who has authorised it, by signing in to the source and consenting. Until recently that flow lived only in the app's own interface. Switching a connector or an action between authorised and unauthorised could be done only there. The access token could be posted back to the provider's secret store only from the provider's domains and the app. And the widget-configuration method would tell your portal the state of each connector, but not the authorisation link to fix it. The federated-connector authorisation flow, in private preview, is the answer for custom portals: the provider's shared backend manages the redirect callback and relays completion over the API, rather than letting a custom interface handle redirects directly. Out-of-the-box actions are out of scope for now. Five components. The customer UI, on the customer's domain. The customer backend, also on their domain, which mediates between the UI and the enterprise API and holds the user's cloud tokens for those calls. The enterprise API, which generates authorisation URLs, exchanges codes for tokens and stores them. A provider-hosted redirect handler page that receives the callback from the third party. And the third-party authorisation server. Eleven steps. The UI loads from the backend. The backend asks the API for the engine's connectors. The API returns them, with an authorisation URL for any the user has not authorised. The UI shows an Authorise button wherever a URL came back. The user clicks, and a pop-up opens the third party's consent flow. After login and consent, the third party redirects to the provider's handler. The handler posts the code and state back to the parent window. The UI calls the backend with them. The backend calls the API to complete, and the API exchanges the code at the third party for a refresh token and an access token. The API reports connector authorised to the backend. The backend reports it to the UI. In practice. Capture the user's cloud token at login, for every API call. Read the widget configuration for each connector's state and authorisation URL. Let the user click through. Call acquire-and-store-refresh-token with the redirect result. Then update the engine's user data to mark the connector authorised, and show Unauthorise once it is.
Three kinds of connector, and the fourth question. An ingestion connector crawls data and access lists from the application, creates a store and an index, and offers search and actions. A federated connector authenticates the end user, uses their access token to retrieve data live, and offers search and actions. A bring-your-own tool-server connector registers a remote server, one you host or one a third party exposes over the tool protocol. You register it by public URL with its authorisation details: authorisation URL, the parameters that request offline access and a consent prompt, token URL, client id and secret, scopes, PKCE. It then behaves as any other data store, usable by a no-code agent for retrieval and actions. Will it replace the ready-made connectors? The slide's table: speed to value, instant and plug-and-play against slower and developer-led (you host it, you build it); data scope, curated and narrow (mail, calendar, documents, incidents, projects) against deep and boundless (proprietary databases, legacy systems, real-time telemetry); maintenance, provider-managed against customer-managed. A ready-made connector is a takeaway meal; a tool server is a fully stocked commercial kitchen, which is wonderful if you like cooking. The fourth question is use-case-centric: do you need a connector at all? A career-coach agent over the HR platform's employee profiles and the internal job postings is a tool server plus the no-code designer. So is a deal advisor over the CRM's opportunities and the historical contract clauses. An agent built with the kit and the integration connectors is also an option.

The support desk: what you can see, and what only the provider can

In plain English: the app is a managed service, so when a customer says the answer was wrong, you may not be able to see why from the outside. The provider's support engineers have an internal debugger that reconstructs any conversation from an assist token. Open the chat, copy its token, raise a support ticket for the enterprise app and quote it. The engineer can then see the final answer, and the grounding and citations: user-visible citations against every source retrieved, with relevance scores. They see the personal context the engine assembled: the most important people, active projects and topics, key concepts and internal vocabulary, significant one-off announcements, important documents, key group aliases, a summary of conversation history, employment information. They see the data connectors in play, and the tool invocation history with the arguments and the size of each response. They see the model execution metrics per caller agent: model, thinking budget, traffic type, prompt, thought and response tokens, prefill cache reads, writes and hits, prefill time, latency. And they see the planner's steps and thoughts in order, and the latency timeline by lane. They do not see your users' content beyond that; they do see enough to tell you which agent, which skill and which tool were used and how many tokens it cost. Response time follows the support tier, from a few hours on a standard channel to much tighter on a premium one. Two corollaries the trainer drew. If the same question is an agent you deployed on the agent runtime, you have all of this yourself in the runtime's traces, and need nobody. And if a customer says the app gives different answers to the same question, that is an evaluation problem, not a defect. An open-source evaluation framework for the app drives its API statelessly in batch. It measures the streaming latency clocks plus grounding and tool-execution latency, and lets you define quality metrics with autograder rubrics or programmatic evaluators. It runs in a container you deploy yourself, with a configuration file per model and setting. So the right use is to compare the app's configurations before you hand one to the customer, the way day three compared agents.

When the panel says… the answer they are listening for

In plain English: each line is a question as a panel would phrase it, then the one-sentence answer that signals you built it, then the reason the usual answer loses.
“Why one enterprise app rather than giving each team its agent?” → The front door: identity, screening, perimeter and logging become properties of the building rather than of each room, a hundred and fifty connectors are already behind it, and an external, customer-facing bot is a different product on a different door.
“Ingest or federate?” → Federate by default: nothing copied, permissions decided live at the source, a 403 passed straight back, days to production. Ingest only where the source has no live, permission-aware API, a static corpus wants an offline index, or a regulator wants the copy under your keys, and then you own the staleness and the access lists.
“Walk me through a query.” → Classify the intent and route, rewrite and split, fan out variants to indexed and federated sources in parallel, collate and de-duplicate by address, rerank against the rewritten query and drop below the threshold, blend the top few into the grounding context, answer with citations and a grounding score; and keep the original query as a variant, because the rewrite is not always an improvement.
“The app takes thirty seconds.” → Read the lanes: the second turn re-reading a fifty-kilobyte tool response is the bill and the wait; cap the response, cache the tool list, run independent steps in parallel, lower thinking on deterministic steps; streaming thoughts moves time to first token and nothing else.
“Which identity should the customer use?” → The provider's directory by default: it supports federated connectors with no access lists, the mobile app, type-ahead sharing and a subject that survives an email change. Federate only for ingestion from the Microsoft suite (group identifiers), a session timeout the free tier cannot give, or an existing federated estate whose migration would reset every user.
“A user changed their email and lost their notebooks.” → The federated pool's subject is the email, so the person became a new principal and the licence, history and shares stayed with the old one; a directory account survives a rename. Immutability is the quiet reason to prefer the directory.
“Group-based access stopped working for one power user.” → Above a hundred and fifty groups the groups claim is redacted from the token entirely; a provisioning sync of users and groups into the pool restores it and, as a bonus, gives sharing its type-ahead.
“The customer needs a four-hour session.” → The free tier of the directory fixes the idle timeout at sixty minutes; configuring it is a premium tier at about six dollars per user per month, so on ten thousand users it is a budget line, and the alternative is federation, where their own provider owns the session.
“Is a Model Armor template on the app enough for the HR spreadsheet?” → No: the response template sees the reply after the document has passed through the engine, the model's context and possibly the sixty-day history. A content policy on the connector blocks or redacts the document at step three, before the model reads it, and it is the only control that sees data arriving through connectors.
“Model Armor keeps blocking harmless replies.” → Disable prompt-injection detection on the response template, set the detectors' confidence to high, use the advanced sensitive-data setting with a custom inspection template at a likely threshold, keep separate templates for prompt and response, and trial changes in inspect-only with logging on.
“The screening missed a six-megabyte PDF.” → Model Armor skips files over four megabytes and fails open, and it does not scan images inside PDFs; a content policy on uploads takes documents to thirty megabytes with text recognition, and the rule from scars is to shard large files before anything reads them.
“They want their own keys.” → A regional location, keys registered in project settings before any data store is created, the key chosen at connector creation and the store assigned to the app, encrypt and decrypt for the service agent; the key on a store can never be changed, rotation breaks a third-party connector today, and it protects what you store, which under federation is little.
“Lock the app down to the office network.” → A service perimeter with an ingress rule for the VPN range, at no extra charge, added after the app exists; never a top-level access level, which overrides the ingress rules; and remember it cannot block web grounding and will block actions until they are allow-listed on egress.
“An admin connected a mailbox nobody approved.” → Org policy, inherited from above: allowed data sources, the custom tool-server constraint enforced everywhere by default, and allowed egress domains; applied at creation, not retroactive, and beyond an admin's reach however many project roles they hold.
“Which logs do we turn on?” → Admin activity is always on; enable data access, the user prompt log with a chosen retention and a sink for longer, and Model Armor's own verdict log for severity and prompt-or-response; and mask before the data lake, because sensitive data is not filtered out of the usage logs.
“Embed the assistant in our portal.” → The streaming assist method through the Discovery Engine API with a licence per user; for federated connectors, your backend holds the user's cloud token, reads the widget configuration for state and authorisation URLs, and completes the flow with acquire-and-store and the user-data update, with the provider's handler receiving the redirect.
“Connector or bring-your-own tool server?” → A ready-made connector for standard objects, instant and provider-maintained; a tool server for proprietary data if the team can host and maintain it, registered with offline access and a consent prompt so a refresh token exists, with the constraint lifted and the domain allow-listed; an agent with the integration connectors as the third option; and the use case decides, not the catalogue.
“The customer says the app gives different answers each time.” → An evaluation problem: drive the app's API in batch through the open-source evaluation framework per configuration, measure consistency, the latency clocks and grounding, and choose a configuration; for a specific bad answer, raise a ticket with the assist token so the provider's engineers can read the trace you cannot.
