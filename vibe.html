Vibe coding: think it, build it, ship it

Vibe coding means describing the app you want in ordinary words and letting an AI tool write it. You say the goal and the feel, the tool writes the code, and you watch a live preview change as you talk. Most people get from an idea to a working preview in about a quarter of an hour. This lab is the skill behind that: how to say what you want so the tool has nothing left to guess, which of Google’s three tools to start in, how to change an app without breaking it, which AI features to add, and how to put it online safely.

Neighbours. When the app you vibe-coded needs to become an agent that uses tools on its own, the ADK lab is the next step. When a coding agent does the building for you at scale, read agents building agents. Words like tokens and context window are taken apart properly in one token at a time.

Say what you want, watch it appear

In plain English: you type something like “build me a habit tracker with a calendar and a streak counter”. An AI tool writes the whole app and shows it running beside the chat. You keep talking to change it. No programming experience is needed to start, though it helps when something breaks.
The analogy for the whole lab. Hiring a very fast builder who never asks questions. Whatever you leave out of the brief, they guess. A one-line brief gets a house; a good brief gets your house. Every tab here is about writing a better brief, choosing the right builder, and checking the work before you hand out the keys.

Try it: build a powerful prompt, one layer at a time

Try this: a lazy prompt is one line: “help me build an app to track my food and water”. It works, but the tool has to guess everything else. A powerful prompt has up to six layers: a role, the goal, the context, whether it needs AI, the vibe, and optionally a visual. Switch the layers on and off. Watch the list of guesses shrink and the preview turn into the app you meant.
in real lifeOrdering a birthday cake by phone. “A cake, please” gets a plain sponge. Say who it is for, how many people, chocolate, no nuts, and a photo of the design you like, and you get the cake you pictured. The baker was always good; the order was the problem.
start byswitch every layer off except the goal and read the guesses. Then add context and AI and watch two guesses disappear and two things appear in the preview.

Three tools, three starting points

In plain English: Google offers three places to vibe-code, and the right one depends on what you have in your hand when you start. A picture of a screen points to Stitch. An idea you want to see fast points to AI Studio. A real engineering task in a folder of code points to Antigravity. Many projects pass through all three in that order.

When someone asks… the short answer

In plain English: each line is a question as a colleague, a client or an interview panel would put it, then the answer that shows you have done it, then why the usual answer falls short.
“What is vibe coding, in one sentence?” → Describing an app’s goal and feel in ordinary words to an agentic tool that writes the code and shows a live preview. You direct and it implements. It is not “no thinking”: the brief still decides the result.
“Why did the tool build the wrong thing?” → The prompt left it guessing. Add the missing layers: a role, the goal, the context of use, whether AI is needed, the vibe, and a visual if you have one. Read the prompt back before you send it.
“Stitch, AI Studio or Antigravity?” → Start from what you hold. A picture of a screen goes to Stitch, an idea to AI Studio, and a task in an existing codebase to Antigravity. Many projects move through all three in that order.
“The tool keeps changing things I did not ask it to change.” → Ask for one specific change at a time, and point at it with the annotate tool when it is visual. Check the action history for files you did not expect. Vague requests touch more files and spend more tokens.
“It forgot the style I asked for at the start.” → The first message has slid out of the context window. Put lasting rules in a saved instruction the tool reads on every turn, such as a skill, a rules file or an artifact, or restate them.
“What is a token, and why should I care?” → A small chunk of text, roughly a short word. Models read, write, bill and limit in tokens. Long chats and big files use them up, and the daily quota is the fuel tank.
“What is MCP for?” → A standard plug that lets an AI tool safely reach your files, your Google Drive or your GitHub code. You connect a source once instead of pasting its contents into every chat.
“Make the app talk about today’s news.” → Search grounding: the model looks up current information before it answers. Without it, the model only knows what it learned during training, and that is always out of date.
“Read the receipt in this photo.” → Image analysis. Image editing is the different feature that changes a photo. Speech generation and transcription are the voice pair: one speaks, the other listens.
“Publish my first app without setting up billing.” → The starter tier publishes from the build tool to Cloud Run with no cloud project or billing account. It is limited to a couple of apps in a single region. Beyond that comes a standard deployment into your own project with billing on.
“I want to keep the code.” → Export it to GitHub, or to Firebase for hosting. The project then outlives the tool, can be reviewed, and can be handed to a developer.
“Is it safe to share the link yet?” → Not until the API key is out of the browser code and a spending cap is set. Also check the failure message, a phone-sized screen, that the sample data is made up, and who the link is shared with.
“Who is vibe coding for?” → Anyone with a real problem. Builders lean on the AI for all of it, and developers use it to go faster and review what it wrote. The difference shows when something breaks: who can read the code.
“What changed that made this possible?” → Models moved from understanding, to thinking, to acting. Agentic AI browses, runs commands and edits files on its own, which is what lets a tool go from a prompt to a published app.
Words in these lines. Agentic: able to take actions on its own. Codebase: all the code files of one project. Annotate: draw on the preview to show what to change. Action history: the list of files changed per request. Context window: how much the AI holds in mind at once. Token: a small chunk of text the AI reads and writes. Quota: how much you may use in a day. MCP: Model Context Protocol, a standard plug to your files and code. Grounding: looking facts up before answering. Cloud Run: Google Cloud’s service that runs an app at a web address. Billing: the account a cloud project charges to. API key: a secret that lets an app spend on your account. Firebase: Google’s app-hosting platform. GitHub: a website for storing code.
